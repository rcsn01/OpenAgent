# The Last Broadcast

The signal had been repeating for three hundred and forty-seven years when Lila first heard it. She was seventeen, huddled in the observation deck of the colony ship *Meridian’s Hope*, her cheek pressed against the cold fusion glass as she stared into the void. The stars did not twinkle out here. They burned with a steady, indifferent light, and somewhere in that darkness, something was calling out.

"It’s old," her grandfather had told her when she was small, his voice rough from the recycled air. "Older than the colony. Older than the exodus. Someone left it running, and we don’t know how to turn it off."

The signal was a simple thing—a sequence of prime numbers, pulsing in binary, followed by a burst of static that almost sounded like music. The elders said it was a navigational beacon from old Earth, a relic from the days before the atmosphere burned and the seas turned to steam. But Lila had listened to it every night for years, and she knew there was more to it than that. There was intention in those notes, a loneliness that mirrored her own.

The *Meridian’s Hope* was not a happy place. It had been built to carry ten thousand souls to a new world, but the cryogenic systems had failed in the second century, and the passengers had woken to find themselves adrift in a ship that was never meant to be a permanent home. Now, after eleven generations, the population had stabilized at around four thousand, but the knowledge of their destination had been lost. The navigation computers had suffered a cascade failure long before Lila was born, and the ship simply traveled onward, following a course no one understood.

Lila worked in the recycling bays, sorting organic waste and reclaiming water from the ship’s closed systems. It was grim work, but it gave her access to parts of the *Meridian’s Hope* that most people never saw. One day, while tracing a faulty moisture condenser, she found a door that had been sealed for centuries. The access panel was corroded, but the manual release still functioned, and with a groan of ancient hydraulics, the door slid open.

Behind it was a room that did not appear on any schematic. It was small, barely larger than a storage closet, but its walls were lined with equipment that predated everything else on the ship. In the center stood a terminal, its screen dark but intact. And in the corner, slumped in a chair, was the body of a man in a uniform Lila did not recognize.

She should have reported it immediately. Protocol demanded it. But something held her back. She approached the terminal and pressed the activation switch. The screen flickered, then bloomed with light, displaying a message in a language she somehow understood: **SIGNAL ORIGIN TRACKING—ACTIVE.**

The man in the chair had been the ship’s original signal officer, tasked with monitoring deep-space communications. He had died at his post, his hand still resting on the console. Lila felt a strange kinship with him. He had been listening, just like her.

The terminal contained records that had been wiped from the ship’s main archive. The *Meridian’s Hope* had not been launched blindly into the void. Its destination had been a planet called Kepler-442b, but the ship had never reached it. A course correction, issued from Earth in the final days before the collapse, had redirected them toward the signal instead. The elders had known. At some point, the knowledge had been suppressed, deemed too dangerous for the colony to bear.

Lila spent weeks returning to the hidden room, teaching herself to operate the ancient console. She learned that the signal was not a beacon. It was a message, broadcast from a station orbiting a black hole at the edge of the observable universe. The station had been built by a coalition of Earth’s last governments, not as a lighthouse, but as a library. It contained the collected knowledge of a dead world, preserved in a place where time moved slowly enough for the data to survive the heat death of the universe.

But there was another message, embedded deep in the noise. A warning. The station was not abandoned. Something had found it before the *Meridian’s Hope* had even left Earth’s orbit. Something that had been waiting for three and a half centuries for someone to answer.

Lila’s discovery did not remain secret for long. She made the mistake of telling her closest friend, a boy named Kael who worked in the hydroponics gardens. Kael told his supervisor, who told the Council of Elders, and within a week, Lila was standing before them in the great amphitheater that served as the ship’s government and court.

"You have disturbed the dead," the First Elder said, his voice trembling with anger or fear, Lila could not tell which. "You have meddled with forces you do not understand. The signal is a trick, a phantom designed to lure the foolish toward destruction."

"It’s a library," Lila said, standing straight despite her terror. "Everything we lost is still there. Our history. Our science. Our stories. We could learn how to fix the navigation systems. We could find out where we’re going."

"We know where we are going," the First Elder replied. "Forward. That is all that matters. The past is ash."

But not everyone agreed. Among the younger generation, Lila’s discovery sparked something that had been dormant for generations: hope. A movement formed, quietly at first, then with increasing boldness. They called themselves the Listeners, and they demanded that the ship alter course to investigate the signal’s origin.

The Council resisted. They had maintained order for centuries through rigid control of information, and they saw the Listeners as a threat to that order. Lila was imprisoned in the recycling bays, forced to work under guard, her access to the hidden room revoked. But the signal still played every night, and now thousands listened with her.

It was Kael who finally freed her. He had grown up in the hydroponics gardens, learning the old skills of cultivation and growth, and he had never accepted the Council’s doctrine of passive endurance. One night, he cut the power to the bay’s surveillance systems and led Lila through maintenance tunnels that only the gardeners knew.

"They’re going to purge the archives," he told her as they crawled through darkness. "The First Elder ordered it. They’re afraid people will find more than that one room. There are other secrets, Lila. Things they’ve hidden about Earth, about why we really left."

They reached the hidden room to find it already occupied. The First Elder stood at the terminal, his fingers moving over the controls with a familiarity that betrayed long practice. He turned as they entered, and in the screen’s glow, Lila saw that he was crying.

"My grandmother was the last signal officer before me," he said quietly. "She died keeping this secret, and I have spent my life doing the same. I told myself it was protection. That you were too young, too fragile, to carry the weight of what she knew."

He stepped away from the console. On the screen, a new message was playing. Not the prime numbers, not the static, but a voice. A human voice, speaking in words that had not been heard on the *Meridian’s Hope* for three centuries.

"This is Station Eternity," the voice said. "We are still here. We are still waiting. If you can hear this, know that you are not alone."

The First Elder looked at Lila, and for the first time, she saw not a tyrant, but an old man exhausted by loneliness.

"I don’t know if it’s true," he said. "I don’t know if anything is still there. But I know we cannot keep running from the past. Will you help me tell them?"

Lila stepped forward and placed her hand on the console. In the silence of the ship, with Kael beside her and the voice of a stranger echoing from the stars, she finally understood why the signal had felt like music. It was not a lure. It was a song, sung into the dark by everyone who had ever been afraid, everyone who had ever hoped that someone, somewhere, was still listening.

"Yes," she said. "But we won’t just tell them. We’ll answer."

And for the first time in three hundred and forty-seven years, the *Meridian’s Hope* sent a message back into the void. Not prime numbers. Not warnings. Just two words, broadcast on a frequency that had only ever carried the voices of ghosts.

**We hear you.**
